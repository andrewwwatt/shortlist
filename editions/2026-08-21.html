Ghost Mail – Dominique Olivier

A word of caution before you employ Claude

Businesses are increasingly rolling out AI and cutting human jobs in the same breath, treating the two as if one simply follows the other. Ghost Mail's Dominique Olivier digs into what that combination actually costs: a new University of Pittsburgh study of more than 3,200 firms found that employee goodwill toward AI collapses the moment AI-linked layoffs are announced – and that goodwill turns out to be one of the strongest predictors of whether the AI actually delivers. Handle the cuts carelessly, in other words, and you can sink the very tool you were trying to justify.

EWN

Rich South Africans are drowning in debt

Proving the old adage that some people have 'more money than sense', DebtBusters' latest quarterly index shows South Africans earning more than R50,000 a month now need 103% of their take-home pay just to service debt – more than they earn, before groceries even enter the picture. The irony is that it's their own creditworthiness to blame: banks are more willing to extend big home loans, vehicle finance and credit facilities to high earners, resulting in more temptation to over-spend.

EWN – Malaika Mahlatsi

The "little treats economy"

With youth unemployment at 33.6% – five million young South Africans currently out of work and out of training – the big milestones (a first home, especially) have quietly stopped feeling reachable for an entire generation. Under-35s make up 37% of the population but own just 7% of residential property. The result, this piece argues, is a "little treats economy": small, deliberate indulgences standing in for the milestones that used to signal you'd arrived. A sharp read on why a generation's spending habits look the way they do.

Loose Canon

A "vibecession" – the recession between your ears

South Africa's economic indicators have been quietly improving – inflation easing, rates holding steady, growth ticking along. So why does everyone still feel broke? Loose Canon calls it a "vibecession": a gap between what the data says and what people actually feel in their bank balance, running on years of accumulated financial anxiety rather than anything currently happening in the economy. A sharp, slightly wry read on why "the economy is fine" never lands the way economists expect.

Financial Mail

How Africa's favourite beers got their names

African beer sales grew faster than anywhere else in the world last year – and the names behind that growth have better stories than the marketing departments probably intended. Madagascar's Three Horses Lager was named for a colonial-era horse-racing town. Chibuku Shake Shake is called that because you genuinely have to shake it before you drink it. A tour through the continent's most-loved beer brands, from Castle to Tusker, and the odd history behind each label.

Business Day – Gugu Lourie

The 1,200-strong pest control team at a Stellenbosch wine estate

While companies elsewhere debate whether AI or robots should take over the workforce, Vergenoegd Löw Wine Estate has stuck with an older solution. Twice a day, 1,200 Indian Runner ducks march through the vineyards eating snails and insects and fertilising as they go – no pesticides, no robots required. The 330-year-old estate is a WWF Conservation Champion and one of the world's Top 100 Sustainable Hotels. Proof the old ways still work.

And if you feel like a real feel-good day out, there's very little that beats watching over a thousand ducks waddle past your table – while you sip on a suitably chilled Chenin, of course.

Ghost Bites – Markets

Ghost Bites – Markets

BHP is the Goliath of JSE mining – a R3.7 trillion giant now leaning on copper, which just delivered more than half its earnings for the first time. DRDGOLD is the David that isn't even fighting the same battle: at R39 billion, roughly 1% of BHP's size, it's stuck stubbornly to gold. But it's just paid a dividend for the 19th year running, something Goliath can't claim, helped by a gold price up 40% over the past year. Different weight classes, same lesson: the smaller player can still quietly win.

In banking, Standard Bank is beating Absa on efficiency. Standard Bank's costs have grown slower than its revenue for ten straight periods (bankers call this "positive jaws"), while Absa faces the opposite this year – costs outpacing income. The gap shows up in the numbers that matter: Standard Bank turns shareholder money into profit at 19.8%, well ahead of Absa's 15.0%. Both banks' reports proudly feature maps of Africa, but both still earn more profit from South Africa alone than the rest of the continent combined.

And then there's Spar, having the kind of week only a supermarket group in turnaround mode can have. Chairman Mike Bosman and deputy chair Dr Shirley Zinn both resigned after months of increasingly public friction with the group's franchisees, sending the share price to a fresh 52-week low. When the people at the top start heading for the exits, it's rarely a great sign for whoever's left holding the trolley.
